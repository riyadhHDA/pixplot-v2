User guide

Using PixPlot v2 in art history

A vision model compares pixels, not archives. This page explains how to read the Atlas scores, what to double-check before drawing conclusions, and how to document a search.

Before you start

PixPlot

Computes neighbourhoods, indexes regions and builds the maps.

An assistant

Queries the corpus, builds comparison plates and suggests leads.

You

Look at the images, verify the sources and make the call.

The default test set (WikiArt) is crowdsourced: half the works have no artist listed, Western painting makes up almost the entire dataset, and dates and locations are missing. Open the Corpus panel (key I) to check those gaps before generalising from a map.

Reading scores and spaces

PixPlot keeps four similarity spaces separate. Checking how a pair behaves across them quickly shows whether two works share a layout, a subject, a palette or brushwork.

DINOv3

Form

Same composition?

Silhouettes, framing and motifs. The best starting point for finding a copy or a reused layout.

SigLIP 2

Subject

Same theme?

What can be put into words: subject, genre, school. Closest to standard art-historical categories.

CIELAB

Colour

Same palette?

Proportions of hues, lightness and saturation, regardless of what is depicted.

DINOv3, layer 6

Touch

Same handling?

Stroke, grain and impasto. Useful for pointillism, prints and drawings.

  • Scores are corpus-relative cosines. They can only be compared within one space: 0.87 in subject and 0.67 in form does not mean "more similar by subject". A distance alone never settles a relationship (Bureau & Doras, 2025).
  • Models also react to the reproduction. Scan grain, yellowed varnish, a frame left in the photograph or white balance can pull two works together. The Corpus panel lists each model's training data and known biases.
  • Probe model bias with conceptual axes. Under the Vue menu, Axes conceptuels (2 mots) projects the corpus onto two SigLIP 2 text prompts (after Impett & Offert's 2D CLIP, 2024). Works far off the diagonal show both the corpus and the model's own habits.
  • Use "Pourquoi ?" and visual measures. In the comparator, Pourquoi ? outlines the matching regions between two images. On each record, Mesures visuelles computes barycentre, symmetry, chromatic entropy and primary split without a neural network (Stork, 2024).
  • Check disagreements. The Disagreements panel (key D) lists pairs that are close in one space and distant in another; those are often the most instructive cases.

Three workflows

The Atlas interface is in French; button labels are translated below.

1. Tracing a filiation or copy

Start from a known painting to see which compositions or copies answer it.

  1. Search for the work (key /, Metadata mode: artist, title or ID) and open its record.
  2. Under Closest works, switch between Form (for a reused layout) and Subject (for the same theme handled differently).
  3. Add candidates to the comparator (Compare, then key C).
  4. Click Pourquoi ? to see which regions actually match across the two images.
  5. If the relationship is backed by a source, record it with Annotate to grow the benchmark, and keep your notes in the Notebook.

Example: on Raphael's Marriage of the Virgin and Perugino's Delivery of the Keys, the "Pourquoi ?" button frames the central temple and the foreground figure groups.

2. Checking a doubtful label

Crowdsourced datasets like WikiArt contain plenty of mislabelled images. PixPlot gathers the ones contradicted by all their neighbours.

  1. Open To check (key V) and pick artist, style or genre.
  2. Compare the painting with its neighbours: it may be a cataloguing mistake, or simply an unusual work in the artist's output.
  3. Log your conclusion in the Notebook (the software never rewrites metadata automatically).

3. Searching for a motif or detail

Find a specific object (halo, skull, blessing hand, drapery) without relying on titles.

  1. With words: pick Described zone mode ("a halo", "a skull", "a hand holding a letter"). This searches across 1.6 million cropped regions.
  2. From an image: open a work's record, click Search a detail and draw a box around the motif.
  3. For a whole scene: switch to Description mode ("a still life with a skull and a candle").

Connecting an assistant (MCP)

The MCP server exposes 19 tools to Claude or any compatible client. The assistant can run searches, view images and cropped details, compare works, compute visual measures, probe 2D conceptual axes and write into your notebook. Its instructions tell it to keep separate what is visible in the image, what the metadata say, what PixPlot computed and its own hypothesis.

A useful prompt looks like: "Among the neighbours of this Annunciation, which ones reuse its layout? Look at the images and say which parts match." Asking directly "Who influenced this painter?" mostly invites speculation outside the corpus.

Setup: docs/MCP.md.

Archiving and citing

  • Notebook (key N): stores your selections and notes locally (exportable as Markdown).
  • Export: builds a Research Pack (.zip) with the images, sourced metadata, scores and full computation provenance (model, parameters, date) so the trail can be audited or repeated.
  • Citation: Riyadh HADJBA, PixPlot v2 : un laboratoire d'exploration visuelle pour l'histoire de l'art, software, Université de Strasbourg, 2026, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24363789 (mention the version used).

Current limits

  • Anything missing from the corpus (WikiArt especially) obviously cannot show up.
  • When an artist heavily transforms a model (e.g. Picasso after El Greco or Delacroix), none of the spaces recovers the filiation; PE-Spatial was tested without success (ARCHITECTURE, § 24).
  • The "Pourquoi ?" button uses a fixed 4×4 grid, so a motif that shifts position or aspect ratio matches less cleanly.
  • Automatic group names are rough suggestions and should be reviewed.
