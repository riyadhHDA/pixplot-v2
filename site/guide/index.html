Guide

The art historian's guide

PixPlot does not say that one work influenced another: it says that a vision model finds them close. This guide explains how to read these proximities, and how to turn them into research leads that you check for yourself.

Before you start

PixPlot

computes, observes and organises.

An assistant

asks questions and makes suggestions.

You

check, interpret and build knowledge.

A computed proximity is a lead, never proof of influence or attribution. The test corpus, WikiArt, is filled in by volunteers: half the works have no named artist, the Western canon dominates, dates and places are missing. The Atlas's Corpus button (key I) takes stock of this: read it before generalising.

Reading a proximity

Four regards, four questions. Two works close in one regard and far apart in another tell you how they are alike.

DINOv3

Form

What layout?

Shapes, composition, motifs. The best for finding a copy or a reused composition.

SigLIP 2

Subject

What does it depict?

What you would describe in words: subject, genre, school. The best for the discipline's categories.

CIELAB

Colour

What palette?

Proportions of hues, lightness and saturation, whatever the subject.

DINOv3, layer 6

Touch

What handling?

Stroke, grain, impasto: useful for pointillism, prints, drawing.

  • The figures are cosines, not probabilities. They only compare within one regard: 0.87 in subject and 0.67 in form do not mean "more alike in subject".
  • A model sees what you do not look at: the grain of a reproduction, a frame, a poor photograph. Each regard has a card (Corpus button): what it was trained on, what it sees, its known biases.
  • Ask "why?". In the comparator, the Pourquoi ? button outlines the zones of the two works that answer each other most. That is where your judgement comes in: a motif, a pose, a composition, or only a similar background?
  • Look for counter-examples. The Disagreements panel (key D) shows pairs that one regard brings together and another keeps apart: often the most instructive.

Three paths

The Atlas interface is in French; button names are translated here.

1. Tracing a filiation

A copy, a quotation, a reused model: start from one work and see what answers it.

  1. Search for the work (key /, Metadata mode: an artist, a title, a number) and open its record.
  2. In Closest works, switch from one regard to another: Form first for a reused composition, Subject for the same theme treated differently.
  3. Set the candidates aside with Compare, then open the comparator (key C).
  4. Click Pourquoi ? (why?): the zones that answer each other are drawn on both images.
  5. If the relationship is attested (a source, a catalogue), record it with Annotate: it joins the benchmark that measures which regard finds what. Keep a trace in the Notebook.

Example: Raphael, Marriage of the Virgin, and Perugino, Delivery of the Keys. The "why" button finds the central temple and, in the foreground, two figures face to face: Joseph slipping on the ring, Christ handing over the keys.

2. Questioning a label

WikiArt's labels are volunteers' suggestions. PixPlot spots those that all the neighbours contradict.

  1. Open To check (key V): for each label (artist, style, genre), the works whose neighbours say something else.
  2. Look at the work and its neighbours: a cataloguing error, or a genuinely singular work? Both exist, and the second is often the more interesting.
  3. Write your conclusion in the Notebook; PixPlot never corrects a label for you.

3. Searching for a motif

A detail rather than a whole work: a halo, a skull, a blessing hand.

  1. With words: Described zone mode, then "a halo", "a skull", "a hand holding a letter". PixPlot searches 1.6 million zones and shows each zone found.
  2. With the image: in a work's record, Search a detail, then draw a frame around the motif. Similar zones in other works appear.
  3. With a description: Description mode for a whole scene ("a still life with a skull and a candle").

Working with an assistant

PixPlot connects to an assistant (Claude, or any model that speaks the MCP protocol) through a server with 16 tools. The assistant can search, look at the images and details, compare, ask why two works look alike, read the groups and write in your notebook. It receives cautionary instructions: distinguish what can be seen, what the metadata say, what PixPlot computes and its own interpretation, and look for counter-examples.

A good question: "Among the works close to this Annunciation, which reuse its composition? Look at them and say what answers what." A bad one: "Who influenced this painter?"

Setup: docs/MCP.md.

Keeping track and citing

  • The notebook (key N) keeps your leads, selections and notes, with the works concerned.
  • Export produces a Research Pack: a .zip with the images, the metadata and their source, the scores and the provenance of each computation (model, parameters, date). Enough to retrace the path.
  • Citing the software: Riyadh HADJBA, PixPlot v2 : un laboratoire d'exploration visuelle pour l'histoire de l'art, software, Université de Strasbourg, 2026, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24363789. Also give the version used.

Known limits

  • The test corpus is WikiArt: what it does not contain, PixPlot cannot find.
  • No regard follows a filiation once the artist strongly transforms the model; a "spatial" model was tried without success (ARCHITECTURE, § 24).
  • The "why" button compares 16 fixed cells: a shifted reuse, or one in another format, shows less well.
  • Group names are a model's suggestions, to be reviewed.
